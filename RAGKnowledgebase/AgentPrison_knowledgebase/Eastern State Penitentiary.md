# Eastern State Penitentiary

This is a case study of the redevelopment of another prison that preserves the history. You can reference this as an example.

---

## 1. Core Historical Facts

- **Founded**: Opened 1829, Fairmount neighborhood, Philadelphia. Designed by John Haviland in "hub and spoke" architecture.
- **The Pennsylvania System**: World's first true "penitentiary." Prisoners subjected to 24-hour solitary confinement to inspire penitence (remorse), not merely punishment. Each cell had its own exercise yard and skylight evoking a church-like setting.
- **Closure & Abandonment**: Closed 1971; remained derelict for nearly two decades. Urban forest grew through cell blocks. Notable former inmates include Al Capone and "Slick Willie" Sutton.
- **Reopening**: Opened as historic site 1994. ~10,000 visitors in hard hats on first year tours.
- **Theoretical significance**: Michel Foucault cited ESP's solitary confinement model in *Discipline and Punish* (1975) as one of the most influential developments in the modern carceral state.

---

## 2. Stabilized Ruin Strategy

**Core concept**: Do not restore, do not sanitize — let decay itself become the narrative medium.

- Preserving the prison in its deteriorated state as a "stabilized ruin" was both a **practical and philosophical decision** (Sally Elk, President & CEO).
- *Practical*: Restoring to 19th-century condition would have been cost-prohibitive; asbestos-laden walls required careful management for 300,000+ annual visitors.
- *Philosophical*: Leaving the site as-is allows visitors to "comprehend the historical layers of the place." The passage of time becomes visible in the building itself.
- Peeling paint, rusted iron doors, and crumbling walls function as **material witnesses** to confined and abandoned lives — the building's own language of rupture.
- Early critics argued that the aesthetic emphasis on the ruin and 19th-century history **distanced the site from its more immediate, problematic recent past** (the mass incarceration era from the 1970s onward).

## 3. Battle Against Developers: Timeline (1971–1994)

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1971 | City acquires title from Commonwealth for $400,000 |
| 1974 | Mayor Frank Rizzo proposes demolition to build a criminal justice center; plan abandoned |
| Mid-1980s | City transfers ESP to Redevelopment Authority; open call for commercial proposals |
| 1988 | Theme park, luxury apartments, hotel, shopping center proposals all submitted — Mayor Wilson Goode rejects all under pressure from preservationists |
| 1991 | Pew Charitable Trusts grant funds stabilization; Halloween Night Tour launched, immediately profitable |
| 1994 | Opens for daily public tours |

**Key 1988 newspaper quote** (*Philadelphia Inquirer*):
> "Mayor Goode derailed a $20 million plan to turn Fairmount's abandoned Eastern State Penitentiary into a shopping center yesterday, saying that development plans would destroy its historic value... Goode apparently caved in to pressure from historical preservationists, who had complained that all three proposals called for tearing down some prison buildings."

## 4. The Eastern State Task Force

**Composition**: Loose coalition of historians, preservationists, and architects including Temple historian Ken Finkel, Mütter Museum's Gretchen Worden, NPS's Bill Bolger, and Sally Elk (later President & CEO for 21+ years).

**Key action**: Given only one day's notice, successfully persuaded Mayor Goode to reject all Redevelopment Authority commercial proposals (April 1988).

**Class profile** (Bruggeman):
> "Rather, these young, highly educated, and predominantly white culture activists — none of whom were Fairmount natives and few, if any, with ties to real prisoners — took it upon themselves to challenge decades of Pollyannaish preservation in Philadelphia."

**Documented failures of inclusion**:
- Repeatedly "forgot" to include community members on committees
- Deliberately delayed projects requiring community input
- 1990s beautification proposal focused on the wealthier Fairmount Avenue side; ignored poorer Brown Street and Corinthian Avenue neighborhoods
- When neighbors reported rampant drug use and prostitution near Brown/Corinthian (1994), Task Force did not act until enraged residents petitioned City Hall directly — over a year later

---

## 5. Race, Class & Urban Renewal Context

- Philadelphia held the **highest incarceration rate of all US cities** as of 2006; Pennsylvania's prison population outpaced all other states, disproportionately incarcerating African Americans.
- **Reagan's War on Drugs** drove sharp increases in Pennsylvania's incarcerated population through the 1980s, simultaneously freeing up real estate in Black neighborhoods.
- Historic-preservation tax credits (available since 1976) and federal home equity loan protections created strong financial incentives for gentrifying historic sites.
- Fairmount was being rebranded by real estate agents as **"the Art Museum Area"** throughout this period.
- Core critique (Leder): ESP's preservation "perfectly situated itself within Philadelphia's urban renewal narrative: displacing minority-filled neighborhoods to make way for the commercial interests of white professionals, who historically did not call Fairmount home."
- Bruggeman: ESP was "perhaps the most perfectly positioned" prison museum to help its community make sense of mass incarceration's urban impact — "and yet so far its efforts to do that have largely failed."

---

## 6. From Neutrality to Institutional Voice

**Pre-2012 problem**: ESP avoided the term "mass incarceration," speaking only of "prisons today" or "contemporary connections." Fear of being perceived as politically partisan undermined purpose.

**2012 turning point**: Sean Kelley (Director of Interpretation) articulated the internal crisis: *"We started saying, how come we're not better at this? Why have we been struggling so much to connect this story to contemporary issues?"* A new interpretive plan followed.

**Rejection of museum neutrality** (Kelley):
> "Museums have this belief that they are neutral spaces and that we should be neutral parties and it's disingenuous. Of course we have roles in these conversations."

ESP consciously steered away from the "voice of God" institutional tone, instead commissioning artists with firsthand connections to incarceration to directly confront audiences.

**2017 Mission Statement**:
> "Eastern State Penitentiary Historic Site interprets the legacy of American criminal justice reform, from the nation's founding through to the present day, within the long-abandoned cellblocks of the nation's most historic prison."

## 7. Major Interpretive Programs & Exhibitions

| Initiative | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **The Big Graph** | 16-ft, 3,500-lb steel sculpture visualizing US incarceration rates since 1900, with racial disparity data and international comparisons. Placed in former baseball field courtyard. |
| **Prisons Today** (May 2016–) | Award-winning permanent exhibition on the catastrophic rise of incarceration since the 1970s; exposes bipartisan War on Drugs failure; ESP's definitive break from neutrality. First exhibit to explicitly state "mass incarceration isn't working" and drop the word "inmate." |
| **Hidden Lives Illuminated** | Animated short films made by currently incarcerated people projected onto ESP's exterior walls. |
| **Returning Citizens Tour Guide Program** | Recently released men and women hired as staff tour guides, bringing firsthand incarceration experience into interpretation. |
| **Voices of Eastern State** | Audio tour narrated by Steve Buscemi, dedicated to former prisoners' life stories. |
